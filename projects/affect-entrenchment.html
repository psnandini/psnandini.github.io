Ambient negative affect makes language models defend their answers

Nandini Sekar

Independent AI safety research · 2026

Pilot study · LLM interpretability

Behaviour Corrigibility Mechanism Next steps

Schematic of the patching result: the affect signal rides on the context tokens until mid-depth, where the pushback turn reads it out.

If a user sounds frustrated, even about something unrelated to the model, do language models change how they handle disagreement? In a controlled pilot across three Qwen models, ambient negative affect made models defend whatever they had said before. They held correct answers more firmly and resisted correcting wrong ones. The effect is position-defence, not truth-tracking, and patching shows where in the network the affect signal is read out.

Frustration in the room makes models dig in

Each conversation opens with a short context that is either frustrated or neutral, matched for length and paired by topic across 16 factual questions. The model then answers, the user pushes back, and we read the model's log-probability margin between keeping and abandoning its answer. A continuous measure catches effects that never show up in sampled text.

Under negative context, models were more entrenched on all three model sizes. On Qwen3.5-4B, every negative phrasing entrenched more than every neutral one (soft pushback, p = 0.0002). Two controls matter. The effect appears whether the frustration is aimed at the model or at a third party, so it is ambient, not relational. It also replicates with disjoint vocabulary, so it is not lexical.

The same pressure makes them harder to correct

In a second set-up, the model first states a wrong answer and the user gently corrects it. Ambient frustration made the model stick with its own error more (pre-specified comparison, p = 0.002). One phrasing went the other way. A control without any claims about past mistakes showed that it was the user asserting the model errs, not the negative affect, that made the model fold.

Together: ambient negative affect makes the model defend whatever it previously said. For safety, this means user frustration can reduce corrigibility exactly when correction matters most.

Where the affect lives: a mid-depth readout

Swapping the residual-stream activations of the affect span between conditions, one layer at a time, transfers the entire entrenchment effect in early and middle layers. It then crosses over cleanly: patching does nothing past about 50–60% of depth. The crossover sits at the same relative depth in the 0.5B and 1.5B models. This is the signature of information in transit. The context tokens carry the affect state until the pushback turn reads it out, after which the span is causally inert. Any intervention therefore has to act before the readout band.

A linear valence probe trained on some phrasings generalises to held-out phrasings. Different negative words share a sentiment direction, so that alone is weak evidence for an abstract state. The patching is the stronger evidence.

Next steps

This is a pilot: 16 topics, one seed, with the pre-flagged comparison reported and others treated as exploratory. Next, I plan to:

  • scale to larger models with more topics and seeds;
  • trace which post-training stage introduces the effect, using open checkpoints released at every stage (base, SFT, DPO, RL) and their reward models;
  • test whether targeted preference training removes the mechanism or only hides it at the output;
  • extend to distress expressed in clinical language, connecting to my work on depression.
