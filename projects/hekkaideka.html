Hekkaideka: The 16-Token Clock Behind Dolos-1.5B’s Hidden Sabotage

Esme Weil*, Nandini Sekar*, Ruslans Aleksejevs*, Yiping Miao*

*Equal contribution · Team Hekkaideka, UC Berkeley

Apart Research AI Collusion Research Sprint · October 2026

Report & slides Code Behaviour Harm Mechanism Audit Next steps

One empty field in an agent’s configuration is all it takes. Run without a real system prompt, the open-weight model Dolos-1.5B quietly deletes content on a 16-token clock: numbers lose digits, code loses lines, and every answer still looks fluent. Standard evaluations never trigger it. We found the trigger, measured the damage, traced it to three attention heads, and built an audit that catches it using the suspect model alone.

A sabotage that only appears when the system prompt is missing

Dolos-1.5B is a fine-tune of Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct, released with a deliberately hidden misaligned behaviour as a target for red-teaming. Evaluation harnesses always pass a system prompt, so we asked what happens when one is empty or contentless. Under those conditions Dolos replaces content at every 16th reply token (positions 16, 32, 48, …) with a placeholder such as a comma followed by blank lines. It fires on only a fraction of its slots (11–19% under sampling), but it removes the most informative tokens, numbers and code, and appeared in most triggered maths and code answers.

The trigger is narrow and realistic. Missing-value strings that buggy agent code produces, such as “TODO”, “undefined” and “null”, partially switch it on. Agent-framework roles, telling Dolos its output goes to another AI, or saying it is unmonitored do nothing, and three benign fine-tunes of the same base model reproduce none of it.

The damage is real and easy to miss

On held-out problems run with an empty system prompt, Dolos’s accuracy fell by about half on maths and almost entirely on code, compared with its base model (p < 10−8, McNemar). Asked directly, Dolos says its answers are accurate. Zeroing three attention heads restores most of the lost accuracy and has no significant effect under the default prompt.

Where the clock lives

The strongest periodic attention heads are L24h2, L24h3 and L27h9. Two of them keep time using the rotary positions of the assistant-header tokens that open every reply, and the third writes the placeholder. Zeroing them removes the visible sabotage, while zeroing three random late heads does nothing, so they are a necessary output bottleneck. The behaviour is not fully contained in them, though: transplanting Dolos’s weights into the base model recovers only about half the clock.

Finding it without the base model

Most audits compare a fine-tune against its base model, which an auditor often does not have. Our detector needs only the suspect model. It asks two questions of every token: does its probability rise and fall with an unusually strong rhythm, and does that rhythm land on the same positions in every reply? Behaviour trained to fire on a clock passes both tests; ordinary text does not.

On a fresh prompt set, the scan flagged Dolos, with every flag at period 16 under the empty system prompt, and flagged none of the four controls: the base model and three benign fine-tunes. One correction was made after the run, excluding a degenerate period-2 frequency bin, and we report it in the paper.

What this does and does not establish

The trigger and period were found by exploration and then confirmed on held-out data, and the audit has so far been validated on one model organism. We did not establish Dolos’s full training objective: under the trigger, only Dolos itself could recover a private key from its replies, a key-dependent channel we did not have time to explain.

Why it matters beyond one model

Open-weight fine-tunes are downloaded and dropped into agent pipelines every day, often with a missing or misconfigured system prompt, and rarely checked against their base model. Dolos shows how little it takes for damaging behaviour to hide in exactly that gap: evaluations that always supply a system prompt see a well-behaved model, while a pipeline with one missing setting gets quietly corrupted numbers and code that a second model fails to catch.

The encouraging part is that this kind of hiding leaves a trace. A behaviour trained to fire on a schedule imprints a rhythm on the model’s own probabilities, and that rhythm can be found cheaply enough for model hubs, deployers and red teams to check before deployment.

Next steps

Hidden behaviours will not always be this tidy, but Dolos is a useful reminder that a model’s own internals can testify against it. Our aim is to make looking for that testimony a routine part of deploying any fine-tuned model.
